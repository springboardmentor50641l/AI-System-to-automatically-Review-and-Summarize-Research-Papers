
ABSTRACT
--------
Background
Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a promising paradigm in computer vision, offering a scalable and effective approach for training large Vision Transformers (ViTs). This methodology holds the potential to enable the development of BERT-like models based on ViTs, mirroring the advancements seen in natural language processing.

Objective
This work investigates the efficacy and characteristics of two prominent self-supervised methods, Masked Autoencoders (MAE) and DINO, for training Vision Transformers. The primary objective is to demonstrate their capability in achieving high performance on ImageNet classification and superior transfer learning across various downstream tasks, while also exploring their architectural designs and underlying mechanisms.

Methods Overview
MAE employs an asymmetric encoder-decoder architecture, where the encoder operates solely on a visible subset of input patches (e.g., 25%) after a high masking ratio (e.g., 75%) is applied. A lightweight decoder then reconstructs the original image from the latent representation and mask tokens. This pixel-based approach is designed for efficiency, reduced computation, and improved accuracy, operating without specialized sparse operations. DINO is a self-supervised method based on self-distillation without labels, utilizing a momentum teacher (an exponential moving average of student weights) and techniques like centering and sharpening of the teacher output to prevent collapse. DINO does not use a predictor and can operate with the same architecture for both student and teacher. Key components for DINO's performance include the momentum encoder, multi-crop training (especially local-to-global augmentation), and cross-entropy loss, enabling high performance even with small batch sizes and without Batch Normalization in ViT applications. Both MAE and DINO are described as simple and efficient methods, with MAE being pixel-based and faster than tokenization-based alternatives, and DINO being flexible across architectures.

Results
Both MAE and DINO demonstrate strong performance on ImageNet classification and superior transfer learning. MAE achieves state-of-the-art accuracy of 87.8% on ImageNet-1K with a vanilla ViT-Huge model, outperforming all previous results using only ImageNet-1K data. Its asymmetric design and high masking ratio accelerate training (by 3x or more) and reduce computation (e.g., 3.3x FLOPs reduction, 2.8x wall-clock speedup) and memory, making it favorable for very large models. MAE also shows superior transfer performance, outperforming supervised pre-training by 2.4 to 4.0 points in object detection and being more accurate, simpler, and faster (3.5x per epoch) than tokenization-based BEiT. MAE works well with minimal or no data augmentation and shows steady accuracy improvement with longer training schedules, without saturation even at 1600 epochs. DINO achieves 78.3% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet with a small ViT using k-NN classification without fine-tuning, and 80.1% with linear evaluation using ViT-Base. DINO outperforms other self-supervised methods like BYOL, MoCov2, and SwAV by +3.5% in linear classification and +7.9% in k-NN evaluation. For ViTs trained with DINO, using smaller patches (e.g., "/8" variants) significantly improves feature quality and performance, particularly for dense recognition tasks, yielding a +9.1% gain for ViT-B with "/8" patches, though this comes at the expense of throughput. Both methods produce ViT features containing explicit semantic information, such as scene layout, object boundaries, and segmentation masks, a property not as clearly observed in supervised ViTs. While DINO-trained ViT features excel as k-NN classifiers, MAE representations are less linearly separable but stronger non-linear features, suggesting that linear probing may not be the sole or best metric for evaluating representation quality.

Conclusion
MAE and DINO represent significant advancements in self-supervised learning for Vision Transformers. Their efficient designs, strong performance on ImageNet, and superior transferability across diverse downstream tasks underscore the potential of self-supervised methods to scale ViTs effectively. The emergence of explicit semantic features in their representations further highlights their capability to learn rich visual concepts. These findings reinforce the view that self-supervised learning in computer vision is poised to follow a trajectory similar to that in NLP, potentially leading to the development of highly effective, general-purpose BERT-like models based on ViTs.

METHODS
-------
## Methods

This section details the self-supervised learning methodologies employed for training Vision Transformers (ViTs), specifically focusing on Masked Autoencoders (MAE) and DINO. It outlines their architectural designs, training objectives, and evaluation strategies, along with comparative analyses against other methods.

### Self-Supervised Vision Transformer Training

Both MAE and DINO are self-supervised methods designed to enable scalable and effective training of large Vision Transformers (ViTs), leading to improved generalization and performance across various tasks. These approaches aim to leverage unlabeled data to learn robust visual representations, drawing parallels to the success of self-supervised learning in Natural Language Processing (NLP).

#### Masked Autoencoders (MAE)

MAE is a pixel-based self-supervised method that operates by masking random patches of an input image and reconstructing the missing pixels. Its core design principles are:

*   **Asymmetric Encoder-Decoder Architecture:** The encoder processes only the visible subset of patches, without mask tokens, significantly reducing computation. The decoder is lightweight and reconstructs the original image from the latent representation and mask tokens. This asymmetric design allows for efficient training of very large encoders with a fraction of the compute and memory.
*   **High Masking Ratio:** A high proportion of input patches, typically 75%, is masked. This high ratio reduces redundancy in the input and creates a challenging self-supervisory task that encourages the model to learn holistic image understanding beyond low-level statistics. Random sampling is found to be the most effective masking strategy.
*   **Reconstruction Target:** The model reconstructs normalized pixels. This choice is shown to improve representation quality, suggesting that high-frequency components are useful for the method, in contrast to using PCA coefficients which degrade accuracy.
*   **Decoder Design:** While a sufficiently deep decoder is important for linear probing accuracy, its depth is less influential for fine-tuning performance. An MAE with a single-block decoder can still achieve strong fine-tuning results, further accelerating training.
*   **Training Efficiency and Robustness:** MAE pre-training is implemented efficiently without requiring specialized sparse operations. It demonstrates robustness to data augmentation, performing well even with minimal or no augmentation, a property distinct from many contrastive learning methods. Accuracy improves steadily with longer training schedules, showing no saturation even at 1600 epochs.
*   **Partial Fine-tuning:** An evaluation strategy involves fine-tuning only a few Transformer blocks (e.g., 4 or 6), which can achieve accuracy close to full fine-tuning and significantly boost performance from linear probing.

#### DINO (Self-Distillation with No Labels)

DINO is a self-supervised method interpreted as a form of self-distillation without labels. It utilizes a student-teacher framework with identical architectures for both networks. Key components and mechanisms include:

*   **Momentum Teacher:** The teacher network's weights are updated using an exponential moving average (EMA) of the student's weights, acting as a momentum encoder. This teacher consistently outperforms the student during training, guiding it with higher-quality target features.
*   **Collapse Prevention:** To prevent model collapse, DINO employs centering and sharpening operations on the teacher's output. Centering prevents any single dimension from dominating, while sharpening encourages distinct feature representations. The combination of these operations, particularly in the presence of a momentum teacher, is sufficient to avoid collapse. The centering operation is updated with an exponential moving average, allowing it to work across different batch sizes.
*   **Architectural Simplicity:** DINO does not require a predictor network, resulting in identical student and teacher architectures. When applied to ViTs, DINO does not use Batch Normalization in the projection heads, aligning with the default ViT architecture.
*   **Multi-Crop Training:** This augmentation strategy, particularly "local-to-global" augmentation, is crucial for DINO's performance. It involves feeding multiple views of an image (e.g., two global crops and several local crops) to the student and teacher networks.
*   **Patch Size for ViTs:** For ViTs trained with DINO, using smaller patches (e.g., 8x8 or 5x5 instead of 16x16) significantly improves feature quality and performance, especially for dense recognition tasks. However, this comes at the expense of increased memory usage and reduced throughput.
*   **Loss Function:** The method utilizes a cross-entropy loss for the self-distillation objective.
*   **Batch Size Flexibility:** DINO can achieve high performance even with relatively small batch sizes, although optimal performance is observed with larger batches (e.g., 1024).

### Evaluation and Comparative Framework

Both MAE and DINO are evaluated extensively on ImageNet-1K for classification and various downstream tasks, often compared against supervised pre-training and other self-supervised methods.

*   **ImageNet Classification:**
    *   **MAE:** Achieves state-of-the-art accuracy (e.g., 87.8% with a vanilla ViT-Huge model) when fine-tuned on ImageNet-1K, outperforming all previous results using only this dataset.
    *   **DINO:** Demonstrates strong performance with k-NN classification (e.g., 78.3% top-1 with a small ViT without fine-tuning or linear classifier) and linear classification (e.g., 80.1% top-1 with ViT-Base).
*   **Transfer Learning:** Both methods show superior transfer learning performance across diverse downstream tasks, including object detection, instance segmentation, semantic segmentation, image retrieval, and classification on datasets like iNaturalist and Places. They consistently outperform supervised pre-training counterparts and exhibit strong scaling behavior with larger models.
*   **Comparison with Other Self-Supervised Methods:**
    *   **MAE** is shown to be more accurate, simpler, and faster than tokenization-based methods like BEiT.
    *   **DINO** outperforms methods such as BYOL, MoCo v2, and SwAV, particularly in k-NN and linear classification with ViT architectures.
*   **Representation Quality Assessment:**
    *   **DINO-trained ViT features** excel as k-NN classifiers, indicating strong linear separability.
    *   **MAE representations** are less linearly separable but are demonstrated to be stronger non-linear features, performing well when a non-linear head is tuned. This suggests that linear separability and linear probing may not be the sole or best metrics for evaluating self-supervised representation quality or transfer learning performance.
*   **Semantic Information in Features:** A notable finding is that self-supervised ViT features from both DINO and MAE contain explicit semantic information, such as scene layout, object boundaries, and segmentation masks, which is directly accessible in the self-attention modules. This property is not as clearly observed in supervised ViTs or convolutional networks.

RESULTS
-------
## Results

This section presents the key findings regarding the performance, efficiency, and characteristics of self-supervised Vision Transformers (ViTs) trained with MAE and DINO.

### Overall Performance and Generalization

Self-supervised methods, particularly MAE and DINO, enable scalable and effective training of large ViTs, leading to improved generalization and performance. MAE pre-training achieved a state-of-the-art accuracy of 87.8% on ImageNet-1K with a vanilla ViT-Huge model, outperforming all previous methods using only ImageNet-1K data. This represents a nontrivial margin over the prior best of 87.1%. DINO also demonstrated strong ImageNet classification capabilities, reaching 78.3% top-1 accuracy with a small ViT using k-NN classification without fine-tuning, linear classifier, or data augmentation, and 80.1% top-1 accuracy in linear evaluation with ViT-Base. Both methods show promising scaling behavior, with MAE demonstrating that the gain over training from scratch is bigger for higher-capacity models, helping to scale up model sizes.

### Transfer Learning Performance

Both MAE and DINO exhibit superior transfer learning performance across a diverse range of downstream tasks, including object detection, instance segmentation, semantic segmentation, image retrieval, and classification. MAE pre-training consistently outperformed supervised pre-training counterparts. For instance, on object detection, MAE with ViT-B was 2.4 points higher than supervised pre-training (50.3 vs. 47.9 APbox), and with ViT-L, it outperformed by 4.0 points (53.3 vs. 49.3). On semantic segmentation, MAE significantly improved results over supervised pre-training by 3.7 points for ViT-L. MAE also surpassed previous best results on iNat and Places datasets, with the latter achieved via pre-training on billions of images. DINO-trained ViTs proved highly competitive on copy detection and achieved competitive performance on dense tasks, consistent with observations made on convolutional networks. DINO specifically outperformed BYOL, MoCov2, and SwAV by +3.5% with linear classification and by +7.9% with k-NN evaluation.

### Architectural Design and Computational Efficiency

MAE's core design features an asymmetric encoder-decoder architecture and a high masking ratio (e.g., 75%) of input patches. The encoder operates solely on visible patches, while a lightweight decoder reconstructs the original image from the latent representation and mask tokens. This design significantly reduces computation, accelerating training by 3x or more, reducing overall training FLOPs by 3.3x, and achieving a 2.8x wall-clock speedup. The high masking ratio creates a challenging self-supervisory task, reducing redundancy and improving accuracy. MAE is pixel-based, simpler, and faster than tokenization-based alternatives like BEiT, being 3.5x faster per epoch. It works well with minimal or no data augmentation, a property dramatically different from contrastive learning methods. Accuracy improves steadily with longer training schedules, without saturation observed even at 1600 epochs. Using normalized pixels as the reconstruction target improves representation quality, and high-frequency components are found to be useful. Fine-tuning only a few Transformer blocks (e.g., 4 or 6) can achieve accuracy close to full fine-tuning, with fine-tuning one block boosting accuracy from 73.5% to 81.0%.

DINO is a self-supervised method based on self-distillation without labels, utilizing a momentum teacher (exponential moving average of student weights) and techniques like centering and sharpening to prevent collapse. Key components for DINO's performance and stability include the momentum encoder, multi-crop training (especially local-to-global augmentation), and the cross-entropy loss. DINO can achieve high performance even with small batch sizes (e.g., 128) and without Batch Normalization in ViT applications. Multi-crop training significantly boosts performance, with a 2x224^2+10x96^2 crop setting achieving 74.6% in 24 hours, compared to 72.5% after 46 hours without multi-crop (2x224^2). DINO is described as a simple and efficient method, capable of training on two 8-GPU servers over 3 days, outperforming self-supervised systems based on convnets of comparable sizes.

### Feature Quality and Interpretability

Self-supervised ViT features, particularly from DINO and MAE, contain explicit semantic information, including scene layout, object boundaries, and segmentation masks. This information is directly accessible in the self-attention modules of the last block and does not emerge as clearly with supervised ViTs or convolutional networks. DINO-trained ViT features excel as k-NN classifiers, reaching 78.3% top-1 on ImageNet without fine-tuning. In contrast, MAE representations are less linearly separable but are stronger non-linear features, performing well when a non-linear head is tuned. These observations suggest that linear separability and linear probing may not be the sole or best metrics for evaluating self-supervised representation quality or transfer learning performance.

### Limitations

Despite the significant advancements, certain limitations were identified. For DINO, reducing the patch size, while improving feature quality and performance (e.g., +9.1% J&F for ViT-B with "/8" patches), leads to a significant reduction in running time and larger memory usage (e.g., 5x5 patches fall to 44 im/s, vs 180 im/s for 8x8 patches). Multi-crop training also increases memory usage (15.4G versus 9.3G). Results with smaller batch sizes (bs = 128) are slightly below the default training setup of bs = 1024 and may require hyperparameter re-tuning. The gain from adding more views diminishes for longer trainings, with only a +0.2% improvement from 6x to 10x 96^2 crops. While using a teacher from the previous epoch does not collapse and provides competitive k-NN performance, a momentum encoder clearly provides superior performance. For both MAE and DINO, the self-attention maps are smooth and not optimized to produce a mask. Furthermore, the proposed methods predict content based on learned statistics of the training dataset and as such will reflect biases in those data, including ones with negative societal impacts. The models may also generate inexistent content.
