ABSTRACT
Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a transformative paradigm in computer vision, offering a scalable and effective approach for training large Vision Transformers (ViTs). This methodology holds the potential to enable the development of BERT-like models based on ViTs, mirroring the advancements seen in natural language processing. This review critically investigates the efficacy and characteristics of two prominent self-supervised methods, Masked Autoencoders (MAE) and DINO, for training Vision Transformers. It analyzes their architectural designs, underlying mechanisms, and training objectives, demonstrating their capability in achieving high performance on ImageNet classification and superior transfer learning across various downstream tasks. Furthermore, this work delves into the unique properties of the features learned by these models, such as the emergence of explicit semantic information, and discusses their computational efficiency, limitations, and implications for future research in self-supervised vision.

INTRODUCTION
The rapid advancements in deep learning have been significantly propelled by large-scale supervised pre-training, particularly in natural language processing (NLP) with models like BERT and GPT. These models leverage vast amounts of unlabeled text data through self-supervised learning (SSL) to learn rich, general-purpose representations, which are then fine-tuned for diverse downstream tasks. In computer vision, while supervised pre-training on large datasets like ImageNet has been highly successful, it is often resource-intensive and limited by the availability of labeled data. The emergence of Vision Transformers (ViTs) has opened new avenues for applying transformer architectures, originally designed for NLP, to visual tasks. However, ViTs typically require extensive data for pre-training to overcome their inductive bias limitations compared to convolutional neural networks (CNNs). This context has spurred significant interest in developing effective self-supervised learning strategies for ViTs, aiming to unlock their full potential by leveraging unlabeled image data.

This review focuses on two highly influential self-supervised methods, Masked Autoencoders (MAE) and DINO (Self-Distillation with No Labels), which have demonstrated remarkable success in training ViTs. These methods represent distinct yet powerful approaches to self-supervision, offering unique insights into how ViTs can learn robust and transferable visual representations without explicit human annotations. This paper aims to provide a comprehensive and critical analysis of MAE and DINO, integrating their architectural innovations, training paradigms, performance metrics, and the qualitative characteristics of their learned features. By synthesizing findings from the original research and contextualizing them within the broader landscape of self-supervised vision, this review seeks to deepen the understanding of their contributions, identify their limitations, and highlight promising directions for future research.

RELATED WORK
Self-supervised learning in computer vision has evolved significantly, moving beyond traditional pretext tasks to more sophisticated approaches that learn representations by solving an automatically generated task from the data itself. Early methods often relied on tasks like image inpainting, colorization, or predicting image rotations. More recently, contrastive learning has dominated the field, with methods like MoCo (He et al., 2020), SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020), BYOL (Grill et al., 2020), and SwAV (Caron et al., 2020) achieving state-of-the-art results. These approaches typically learn representations by maximizing agreement between different augmented views of the same image while minimizing agreement with views from other images. While highly effective, many contrastive methods are sensitive to data augmentation strategies and often require large batch sizes or memory banks.

The success of masked language modeling in NLP, exemplified by BERT (Devlin et al., 2019), inspired a new wave of self-supervised methods for vision based on masked image modeling (MIM). Approaches like BEiT (Bao et al., 2021) and SimMIM (Xie et al., 2022) apply masking strategies to image patches and train a model to reconstruct the masked content, often using discrete visual tokens or raw pixels. These methods directly translate the masking paradigm to vision, demonstrating its efficacy for learning powerful representations, particularly for ViTs.

MAE (He et al., 2021) builds upon the masked image modeling concept but introduces a highly asymmetric encoder-decoder architecture and a very high masking ratio, significantly enhancing efficiency and scalability. DINO (Caron et al., 2021), on the other hand, represents a distinct approach rooted in self-distillation, where a student network learns by matching the output of a momentum-updated teacher network, without relying on explicit masking or negative pairs. Both MAE and DINO have emerged as leading methods, each offering unique advantages and contributing significantly to the understanding of self-supervised ViT training. This review will delve into their specific mechanisms and contributions in detail.

ARCHITECTURAL INNOVATIONS AND TRAINING PARADIGMS
Both MAE and DINO introduce distinct architectural designs and training objectives that enable scalable and effective training of large Vision Transformers (ViTs), leading to improved generalization and performance across various tasks. These approaches aim to leverage unlabeled data to learn robust visual representations, drawing parallels to the success of self-supervised learning in Natural Language Processing (NLP).

### Masked Autoencoders (MAE)
MAE is a pixel-based self-supervised method that operates by masking random patches of an input image and reconstructing the missing pixels (He et al., 2021). Its core design principles are engineered for efficiency and representation quality:

*   **Asymmetric Encoder-Decoder Architecture:** A key innovation of MAE is its asymmetric design. The encoder processes only the visible subset of patches, without mask tokens, significantly reducing computation. This design choice is critical because it allows for the efficient training of very large encoders with a fraction of the compute and memory typically required for full image processing. The decoder, in contrast, is lightweight and reconstructs the original image from the latent representation and mask tokens. This asymmetry is a deliberate design to focus computational resources on learning robust representations in the encoder, while offloading the reconstruction task to a simpler decoder.
*   **High Masking Ratio:** MAE employs a high proportion of masked input patches, typically 75%. This high ratio is not arbitrary; it reduces redundancy in the input, forcing the model to learn holistic image understanding beyond low-level statistics. The challenging nature of reconstructing a large portion of the image encourages the encoder to capture more abstract and semantic features. Random sampling is found to be the most effective masking strategy, suggesting that the task's difficulty, rather than specific spatial patterns, is paramount.
*   **Reconstruction Target:** The model reconstructs normalized pixels, rather than raw pixel values or other representations like PCA coefficients. This choice is shown to improve representation quality, suggesting that high-frequency components, which are preserved through normalization, are useful for the method to learn fine-grained details and robust features.
*   **Decoder Design Flexibility:** While a sufficiently deep decoder is important for achieving high linear probing accuracy, its depth is less influential for fine-tuning performance. An MAE with a single-block decoder can still achieve strong fine-tuning results, further accelerating training. This flexibility allows for tailoring the decoder complexity based on the specific evaluation metric or downstream task.
*   **Training Efficiency and Robustness:** MAE pre-training is implemented efficiently without requiring specialized sparse operations, making it practical for large-scale training. It demonstrates remarkable robustness to data augmentation, performing well even with minimal or no augmentation, a property distinct from many contrastive learning methods that heavily rely on diverse augmentations. Furthermore, accuracy improves steadily with longer training schedules, showing no saturation even at 1600 epochs, indicating its capacity for continuous learning and scalability with increased training time.
*   **Partial Fine-tuning:** An evaluation strategy involves fine-tuning only a few Transformer blocks (e.g., 4 or 6) of the pre-trained encoder. This approach can achieve accuracy close to full fine-tuning and significantly boost performance from linear probing, highlighting the quality of the learned representations and the efficiency of adapting them to downstream tasks.

### DINO (Self-Distillation with No Labels)
DINO is a self-supervised method interpreted as a form of self-distillation without labels, utilizing a student-teacher framework with identical architectures for both networks (Caron et al., 2021). Its mechanisms are designed to prevent model collapse and encourage the learning of rich, discriminative features:

*   **Momentum Teacher:** The teacher network's weights are updated using an exponential moving average (EMA) of the student's weights, acting as a momentum encoder. This mechanism ensures that the teacher provides a stable and consistently higher-quality target for the student during training, guiding it towards better feature representations. The momentum update prevents the teacher from becoming too similar to the student too quickly, maintaining a beneficial asymmetry in learning.
*   **Collapse Prevention:** To prevent model collapse—a common issue in self-supervised learning where the model learns trivial, constant representations—DINO employs centering and sharpening operations on the teacher's output. Centering prevents any single dimension from dominating the feature space, effectively regularizing the teacher's output. Sharpening, conversely, encourages distinct feature representations by increasing the entropy of the teacher's predictions. The combination of these operations, particularly in the presence of a momentum teacher, is sufficient to avoid collapse. The centering operation is updated with an exponential moving average, allowing it to work effectively across different batch sizes.
*   **Architectural Simplicity:** DINO does not require a predictor network, resulting in identical student and teacher architectures. This simplifies the model design and reduces computational overhead. When applied to ViTs, DINO does not use Batch Normalization in the projection heads, aligning with the default ViT architecture and demonstrating its compatibility with standard transformer designs.
*   **Multi-Crop Training:** This augmentation strategy, particularly "local-to-global" augmentation, is crucial for DINO's performance. It involves feeding multiple views of an image (e.g., two global crops and several local crops) to the student and teacher networks. This forces the model to learn representations that are invariant to scale and local distortions, enhancing its robustness and generalization capabilities.
*   **Patch Size for ViTs:** For ViTs trained with DINO, using smaller patches (e.g., 8x8 or 5x5 instead of 16x16) significantly improves feature quality and performance, especially for dense recognition tasks. This suggests that finer-grained local information is beneficial for DINO's self-distillation process, although this comes at the expense of increased memory usage and reduced throughput.
*   **Loss Function:** The method utilizes a cross-entropy loss for the self-distillation objective, measuring the similarity between the student's and teacher's output distributions.
*   **Batch Size Flexibility:** DINO can achieve high performance even with relatively small batch sizes (e.g., 128), although optimal performance is observed with larger batches (e.g., 1024). This flexibility makes DINO more accessible for researchers with limited computational resources compared to some contrastive methods that are highly sensitive to batch size.

PERFORMANCE AND TRANSFERABILITY
Both MAE and DINO have demonstrated exceptional performance across various benchmarks, showcasing their ability to learn robust and transferable visual representations. Their evaluation strategies typically involve fine-tuning on ImageNet-1K for classification and assessing transfer learning capabilities on a diverse set of downstream tasks.

### ImageNet Classification Performance
MAE pre-training has achieved state-of-the-art accuracy on ImageNet-1K when fine-tuned, demonstrating its superior representation learning capabilities. For instance, MAE achieved 87.8% top-1 accuracy with a vanilla ViT-Huge model, outperforming all previous results using only ImageNet-1K data (He et al., 2021). This represents a nontrivial margin over the prior best of 87.1%, highlighting the effectiveness of its masked image modeling approach. The method also shows promising scaling behavior, with the gain over training from scratch being more significant for higher-capacity models, which is crucial for scaling up model sizes effectively.

DINO also demonstrates strong ImageNet classification capabilities. With a small ViT, it reached 78.3% top-1 accuracy using k-NN classification without fine-tuning, a linear classifier, or extensive data augmentation (Caron et al., 2021). Furthermore, DINO achieved 80.1% top-1 accuracy in linear evaluation with a ViT-Base model. These results underscore DINO's ability to learn linearly separable features, which is often a strong indicator of representation quality.

### Superior Transfer Learning
A significant strength of both MAE and DINO lies in their superior transfer learning performance across a diverse range of downstream tasks, including object detection, instance segmentation, semantic segmentation, image retrieval, and classification on datasets like iNaturalist and Places.

MAE pre-training consistently outperformed supervised pre-training counterparts. For object detection, MAE with ViT-B was 2.4 points higher than supervised pre-training (50.3 vs. 47.9 APbox), and with ViT-L, it outperformed by 4.0 points (53.3 vs. 49.3) (He et al., 2021). On semantic segmentation, MAE significantly improved results over supervised pre-training by 3.7 points for ViT-L. MAE also surpassed previous best results on iNat and Places datasets, with the latter achieved via pre-training on billions of images, indicating its strong generalization across different domains and scales. The consistent outperformance over supervised pre-training suggests that MAE learns more robust and generalizable features, possibly due to its focus on holistic image understanding through reconstruction.

DINO-trained ViTs also proved highly competitive on tasks like copy detection and achieved strong performance on dense tasks, consistent with observations made on convolutional networks (Caron et al., 2021). DINO specifically outperformed other prominent self-supervised methods such as BYOL, MoCo v2, and SwAV by +3.5% with linear classification and by +7.9% with k-NN evaluation, particularly when applied to ViT architectures. This highlights DINO's effectiveness in learning highly discriminative features suitable for various recognition tasks.

### Computational Efficiency
MAE's asymmetric design and high masking ratio contribute significantly to its computational efficiency. The encoder operates solely on visible patches, while a lightweight decoder reconstructs the original image. This design significantly reduces computation, accelerating training by 3x or more, reducing overall training FLOPs by 3.3x, and achieving a 2.8x wall-clock speedup (He et al., 2021). MAE is also described as pixel-based, simpler, and faster than tokenization-based alternatives like BEiT, being 3.5x faster per epoch. Its robustness to minimal or no data augmentation further streamlines the training process.

DINO, while not as explicitly focused on speedup through architectural asymmetry, is described as a simple and efficient method. It can achieve high performance even with small batch sizes (e.g., 128), though optimal performance is with larger batches (e.g., 1024) (Caron et al., 2021). Multi-crop training, while boosting performance, does increase computational load; for example, a 2x224^2+10x96^2 crop setting achieved 74.6% in 24 hours, compared to 72.5% after 46 hours without multi-crop (2x224^2). DINO is capable of training on two 8-GPU servers over 3 days, outperforming self-supervised systems based on convnets of comparable sizes.

FEATURE INTERPRETABILITY AND REPRESENTATION QUALITY
Beyond quantitative performance metrics, a critical aspect of self-supervised learning is the quality and interpretability of the learned representations. Both MAE and DINO have revealed fascinating insights into how ViTs process visual information when trained without labels.

### Emergence of Explicit Semantic Information
A notable and highly significant finding is that self-supervised ViT features, particularly from DINO and MAE, contain explicit semantic information. This includes scene layout, object boundaries, and even segmentation masks, which are directly accessible in the self-attention modules of the last block (Caron et al., 2021; He et al., 2021). This property is not as clearly observed in supervised ViTs or traditional convolutional networks, where such semantic understanding often requires specialized architectures or explicit supervision. The emergence of these rich, interpretable features suggests that the self-supervisory tasks (masking and reconstruction for MAE, self-distillation for DINO) compel the ViT to learn a deep, compositional understanding of visual scenes, rather than merely surface-level statistics. This capability hints at the potential for these models to serve as powerful, general-purpose visual backbones.

### Representation Quality Assessment
The methods also offer different perspectives on what constitutes a "good" representation. DINO-trained ViT features excel as k-NN classifiers, reaching 78.3% top-1 on ImageNet without fine-tuning (Caron et al., 2021). This indicates strong linear separability of the learned features, meaning that simple linear classifiers can effectively distinguish between different classes. This property is often highly valued as it suggests that the features are well-organized and discriminative in a linear space.

In contrast, MAE representations are less linearly separable but are demonstrated to be stronger non-linear features, performing exceptionally well when a non-linear head is tuned (He et al., 2021). This observation suggests that linear separability and linear probing, while convenient metrics, may not be the sole or best indicators for evaluating self-supervised representation quality or transfer learning performance, especially for methods that encourage learning more complex, non-linear relationships. The strength of MAE's non-linear features implies that its representations encode information in a way that benefits from more sophisticated downstream classifiers, potentially capturing richer, more abstract relationships that are not easily disentangled by a simple linear projection. This distinction highlights a crucial theoretical insight: different self-supervised objectives can lead to representations with varying characteristics, each potentially optimal for different types of downstream tasks or evaluation protocols.

LIMITATIONS AND CHALLENGES
Despite the significant advancements brought by MAE and DINO, certain limitations and challenges have been identified, which warrant further research and development.

For DINO, while reducing the patch size (e.g., 8x8 or 5x5 instead of 16x16) significantly improves feature quality and performance (e.g., +9.1% J&F for ViT-B with "/8" patches), this comes at a considerable cost to computational efficiency (Caron et al., 2021). Smaller patches lead to a significant reduction in running time (e.g., 5x5 patches fall to 44 im/s, vs 180 im/s for 8x8 patches) and larger memory usage (e.g., multi-crop training increases memory usage from 9.3G to 15.4G). This trade-off between performance and computational resources presents a practical challenge for deploying DINO-trained models, especially in resource-constrained environments. Additionally, while DINO can operate with smaller batch sizes (e.g., 128), results are slightly below the default training setup of batch size 1024 and may require hyperparameter re-tuning, indicating a sensitivity to batch size for optimal performance. The gain from adding more views in multi-crop training also diminishes for longer training schedules, with only a +0.2% improvement from 6x to 10x 96^2 crops, suggesting diminishing returns for increased augmentation complexity. Furthermore, while using a teacher from the previous epoch does not collapse and provides competitive k-NN performance, a momentum encoder clearly provides superior performance, underscoring the importance of this specific mechanism.

For both MAE and DINO, a common observation is that the self-attention maps, while containing explicit semantic information, are smooth and not explicitly optimized to produce sharp segmentation masks (Caron et al., 2021; He et al., 2021). While they reveal object boundaries and scene layouts, achieving precise pixel-level segmentation might require additional fine-tuning or specialized decoder architectures.

A broader ethical concern, applicable to any model trained on large datasets, is that the proposed methods predict content based on learned statistics of the training dataset and as such will reflect biases present in those data, including ones with negative societal impacts (He et al., 2021). The models may also generate inexistent content or hallucinate details during reconstruction (for MAE) or feature generation (for DINO), which could be problematic in sensitive applications. Addressing these biases and ensuring the reliability and safety of generated content remains a critical challenge for the broader self-supervised learning community.

FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS
The advancements made by MAE and DINO open several promising avenues for future research, building upon their strengths and addressing identified limitations.

One key direction involves **optimizing computational efficiency** for high-resolution and dense prediction tasks. While smaller patch sizes improve DINO's feature quality, the associated computational overhead is substantial. Future work could explore more efficient architectures or training strategies that allow for fine-grained feature learning without prohibitive resource demands. This might include hierarchical masking strategies for MAE or adaptive multi-crop sampling for DINO.

Another area is **deeper theoretical understanding** of the mechanisms preventing collapse and promoting representation learning. While centering and sharpening are effective for DINO, and the high masking ratio for MAE, a more comprehensive theoretical framework could lead to novel, more robust, and universally applicable collapse prevention techniques. Investigating the interplay between different self-supervisory signals and their impact on the linear vs. non-linear separability of learned features is also crucial.

**Extending to other modalities and tasks** beyond standard image classification and dense prediction is a natural progression. Applying MAE and DINO principles to video, 3D data, or even multimodal learning (e.g., vision-language pre-training) could unlock new capabilities. For instance, adapting masked autoencoding for video could involve masking spatio-temporal patches, while DINO could be extended to learn joint representations across modalities.

**Mitigating biases and enhancing trustworthiness** is an ethical imperative. Future research should focus on developing methods to detect, quantify, and mitigate biases embedded in self-supervised models due to training data. Techniques for ensuring the factual accuracy and safety of generated content, especially for MAE's reconstruction capabilities, are also vital. This could involve incorporating fairness-aware objectives during pre-training or developing robust post-hoc bias correction mechanisms.

Finally, exploring **hybrid self-supervised approaches** that combine the strengths of different paradigms could yield even more powerful models. For example, integrating aspects of masked image modeling with self-distillation or contrastive learning might lead to representations that are both linearly separable and rich in non-linear information, offering the best of both worlds for diverse downstream applications. Further investigation into the optimal training schedules, augmentation strategies, and architectural choices for these hybrid models will be essential.

CONCLUSION
MAE and DINO represent significant advancements in self-supervised learning for Vision Transformers, fundamentally reshaping how large-scale visual representations are learned. MAE's efficient asymmetric encoder-decoder architecture and high masking ratio enable scalable pre-training, yielding state-of-the-art performance and superior transferability with remarkable computational efficiency. DINO, through its self-distillation framework with a momentum teacher and collapse prevention mechanisms, learns highly discriminative features that excel in linear separability and transfer across various tasks. Both methods demonstrate the remarkable emergence of explicit semantic information within their self-attention modules, a property not as clearly observed in supervised counterparts, underscoring their capability to learn rich, interpretable visual concepts.

While challenges remain, particularly concerning computational trade-offs for fine-grained features and the inherent biases from training data, the profound impact of MAE and DINO on the field is undeniable. Their efficient designs, strong performance, and superior transferability reinforce the view that self-supervised learning in computer vision is poised to follow a trajectory similar to that in NLP, potentially leading to the development of highly effective, general-purpose BERT-like models based on ViTs. Future research will undoubtedly build upon these foundational methods, addressing current limitations and exploring new frontiers in representation learning for a wide array of visual tasks.
