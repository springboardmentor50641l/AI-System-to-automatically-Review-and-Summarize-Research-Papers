Recent advancements in deep learning architectures have significantly impacted image classification tasks. One notable development is the Inception-v3 architecture, which has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on the ILSVRC 2012 classification challenge. For single-crop/single-frame evaluation, this architecture achieved a Top-1 Error of 21.2% and a Top-5 Error of 5.6%. This performance was reported to be achieved with a computational cost of 5 billion multiply-adds per inference and utilizing fewer than 25 million parameters.

Further improvements in performance were observed with multi-crop evaluation strategies. Inception-v3 models, when evaluated with multi-crop techniques, achieved 18.77% Top-1 Error and 4.2% Top-5 Error for a single model. The application of ensembles of Inception-v3 models with multi-crop evaluation yielded even more enhanced results, reaching 17.2-17.3% Top-1 Error and 3.5-3.58% Top-5 Error. It is noted that while most of these ensemble results were reported on the validation set, the top-5 ensemble result specifically yielded 3.46% error on the validation set.

Beyond its performance metrics, the Inception-v3 solution is characterized by its computational efficiency. It reportedly requires significantly less computation and fewer parameters compared to other state-of-the-art denser networks. One study indicated that this model outperformed certain denser networks by reducing top-5 error by 25% and top-1 error by 14% relatively, while being six times cheaper computationally and using at least five times fewer parameters (an estimated figure). The design principles, which include a lower parameter count combined with regularization techniques such as batch-normalized auxiliary classifiers and label-smoothing, facilitate the training of high-quality networks even on relatively modest-sized training datasets. The development of this architecture involved the description of general principles and optimization ideas for efficiently scaling up convolutional networks. Additionally, research suggests that high-quality results can be achieved with lower-resolution networks, with the final result quality being quite close to that of their higher-resolution counterparts when computational effort is kept constant, although lower-resolution networks may require longer training times.
